A plugin for Claude Code

Maestro

He runs the work. You keep the decisions.

A butler for your session — he sizes each job, dispatches it to one of thirty workers, keeps the record on disk, and taps your shoulder only when the decision is genuinely yours.

Install GitHub

One session stays light enough to drive for hours. Everything else is delegated, isolated, reviewed by a rival family, and written down.

Panic is a scheduling error. I don’t schedule it.

01 The Roster

Thirty workers. None of them you.

The one you talk to never touches the code. That is the whole trick — one mind keeps the shape, and no worker has to hold anybody else’s brief in its head. The roster ships fixed: no one joins mid-run, and the one you actually talk to is forbidden from implementing. There is a law against it.

Every brief carries one of five classes — recon, mechanical, standard, expert, apex. The class decides which model does the work, at what effort, and which reviewer is strong enough to judge the result.

02 The Record

Not in the record? It didn’t happen.

He is severe on exactly one point. .maestro/ledger.jsonl is append-only and it is the session’s memory. “Tests pass” is not a sentence someone says; it is a command that ran, with exit code 0, stamped by the only script permitted to stamp it. A claim without a stamp is gossip.

.maestro/ledger.jsonlappend-only
01 {"seq":41,"kind":"mission-open","payload":{"mission_id":"m-014","title":"rate-limit /export"}}
02 {"seq":42,"kind":"route","payload":{"phase":"author","task_class":"standard","resolved_seat":"executor-claude-standard","reserved_review":{"seat":"reviewer-gemini"}}}
03 {"seq":43,"kind":"gate","payload":{"cmd":"npm test","exit_code":1}}
04 {"seq":44,"kind":"revise-verdict","payload":{"seat":"reviewer-gemini","detail":"round 1"}}
05 {"seq":45,"kind":"gate","payload":{"cmd":"npm test","exit_code":0}}
06 {"seq":46,"kind":"stop","payload":{"stop_state":"DONE","reporter":"maestro"}}

Six lines. The whole morning. Line 02 was written before the worker existed — who would do the job and who would judge it, decided and recorded first. Line 03 is in there on purpose: the record keeps failures at the same volume as successes. Every line also carries a stamped ts, correlation_id and schema_version, trimmed here for width.

Two other parts of the record

Your decision drawer
Decisions that are genuinely yours pile up neatly in holds.jsonl. Never lost, never nagging, never mixed in with the work.
The first read
He reads the record once, aloud, at session start — then does not re-read it. Neither should you.

The stop vocabulary is closed: DONE, BLOCKED-OPERATOR, QUOTA-WAIT, BUDGET-CEILING, EXHAUSTED. A stop narrated but not recorded didn’t happen.

03 Recovery

The record survives the night.

Send every worker home at midnight. Close the laptop mid-sentence, run out of context, kill the session in a temper — it makes no difference. At dawn new workers read the record, reconcile the roster against what is actually still running, and resume at the exact step where work stopped. Restart is reconciliation, not re-planning.

Why it can do that

Every executor commits after each coherent step in its own worktree and writes one line to progress.jsonl:

{ step, done_evidence, next }

So the cost of a crash is bounded by how finely the work was stepped — never by how large the job was.

04 Review

No worker reviews its own work.

Whoever wrote it does not get to approve it. The reviewer comes from a different model family than the author, and the script that closes a mission derives both families from the routing config rather than believing what the record claims about itself. A same-family review reported as independent does not close. The floor is locked in settings; it cannot be lowered.

When no rival family is reachable at all, work is not held hostage to it: review drops to a fresh Claude reviewer with no access to the author’s transcript, usually on a different Claude model. That is a lower floor and it is named as one — recorded as degraded-path, counted separately, never called cross-family.

And when the roster disagrees

  1. 1 The reviewer says revise. The same worker resumes — same session, same memory. Twice at most.
  2. 2 Still failing on quality: the job moves up one rung to a stronger model, once per mission and never twice. A CLI that fell over is not a quality failure — that reroutes at the same rung and costs nothing.
  3. 3 Still stuck: two families with no stake in the dispute take independent positions, then reconcile. Two passes, hard cap.
  4. 4 Small disagreements park in the drawer with their evidence. Only a foundational one — S1 — reaches you, with both positions verbatim.

Ambiguity about what you meant skips the ladder entirely and comes straight to your chair, as one question.

05 Install

Two commands. Then ask for something.

1 Engage him.

claude plugin marketplace add Fredasterehub/maestro claude plugin install maestro@maestro

Installed once, at user scope — every session is met at the door. claude plugin update maestro when a new version lands.

2 Clear the field.

Remove any competing orchestration layer from your global CLAUDE.md first. Two orchestration layers in one session will spend it arguing about who is in charge.

3 Ask for something.

There is no setup interview. State lands in .maestro/ on first use; /maestro:doctor will tell you the truth about your install, read-only.

  • /maestro:statusneeds-you · done · self-progressing · queued
  • /maestro:handoffsweep session knowledge somewhere durable
  • /maestro:doctorinstall and state health
  • /maestro:auditprocess health: revise rates, first-pass rate by class, degraded closes, worker deaths

06 The Trees

Every job gets its own worktree.

No work happens in the checkout you are sitting in. Each job is dispatched into an isolated worktree and nothing lands until a reviewer from another family signs for it.

The close then proves the landing instead of trusting it. Your branch has to carry the exact commit that was reviewed, or a squash with that commit’s patch identity — nothing else may ride in under the same signature, and a repository with uncommitted changes does not close at all. The second diagram is the one that decides whether you are disturbed at all.

Dispatch. Don’t implement.
